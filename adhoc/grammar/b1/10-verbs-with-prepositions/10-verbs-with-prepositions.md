# Verbs with Prepositions

## What is this about?

Many German verbs come with a **fixed preposition**. You already met two of them in [reflexive verbs](../../a2/60-reflexive-verbs.md):

- Ich freue mich **auf** den Urlaub. (I'm looking forward to the holiday.)
- Erinnerst du dich **an** mich? (Do you remember me?)

There are many more, and you need them in almost every conversation.

## Compare with English

English does this too: you wait **for** someone, you listen **to** music, you depend **on** something. Nobody chooses these prepositions. You just know them.

The problem is that the German preposition is often **not the one you'd guess**:

| English         | German            | Word-for-word       |
| --------------- | ----------------- | ------------------- |
| wait **for**    | warten **auf**    | wait on             |
| think **of**    | denken **an**     | think at            |
| be interested **in** | sich interessieren **für** | interest oneself for |
| afraid **of**   | Angst haben **vor** | have fear before  |

## The big rule: learn the verb and preposition together

The preposition here has lost its normal meaning. "auf" in "warten auf" doesn't mean "on top of". So don't translate it, and don't try to work it out. Learn it as one piece, like you learn der/die/das with a noun:

- ~~warten~~ → **warten auf** + accusative
- ~~denken~~ → **denken an** + accusative

## Why it matters

These verbs are how you talk about what you're thinking, what you want, and what you're interested in.

- **Worauf** wartest du? (What are you waiting for?)
- Ich habe keine Lust **darauf**. (I don't feel like it.)
- **Das kommt darauf an.** (It depends.)

You will hear these all the time, and getting them right makes you sound much more natural.
