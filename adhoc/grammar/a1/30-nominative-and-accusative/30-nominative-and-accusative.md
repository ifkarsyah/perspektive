# Nominative and Accusative

## What is this about?

German words change depending on their **job in the sentence**. These jobs are called **cases**. At A1 you meet the first two:

- **Nominative** = the one **doing** the action (the subject).
- **Accusative** = the one the action is **done to** (the direct object).

## Compare with English

English does this too, but only with pronouns:

- **He** sees **me**. (not "Him sees I")
- **I** see **him**.

"He" changes to "him" when it moves from doing the action to receiving it. That's exactly what cases are!

German does the same thing, but also with **articles** (der, ein, mein...):

- **Der** Mann sieht den Hund. (**The** man sees the dog.)
- Der Hund sieht **den** Mann. (The dog sees **the** man.)

Same man, but "der" became "den" because he's now the one being seen.

## How to find which is which

Ask two questions about the sentence:

1. **Who is doing it?** → nominative (subject)
2. **What or who is it being done to?** → accusative (direct object)

Example: "Der Mann kauft den Tisch." (The man buys the table.)

1. Who is buying? The man → **der Mann** = nominative
2. What is being bought? The table → **den Tisch** = accusative

## The great news

At this level, **only masculine changes**. Feminine, neuter, and plural look the same in both cases.

| Case          | masc.     | fem.    | neut.   | plural  |
| ------------- | --------- | ------- | ------- | ------- |
| Nominative    | der       | die     | das     | die     |
| Accusative    | **den**   | die     | das     | die     |

So the only thing to remember: **der → den** when it's the object. Everything else stays the same.

## Why German needs this

Because German word order is flexible (remember [verb in second position](14-verb-second-position.md)?), the case tells you who's doing what:

- **Den** Mann sieht der Hund. → The dog sees the man.

Even though "Mann" comes first, "**den**" tells you he's the object. The dog is still the one seeing. English can't do this; it relies on word order.
